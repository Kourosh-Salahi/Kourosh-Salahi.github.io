Part 1. Neural field fitting in 2D

Model architecture

For image fitting, I used a fully-connected network with width 256 and positional encoding L = 10. The model takes the 2D coordinates (after positional encoding, dimension 4L + 2) and passes them through three hidden layers:

  • Input: 4L + 2-dimensional positionally encoded coordinates
  • Hidden layers: 3 × (Linear, ReLU), each of width 256
  • Output layer: Linear, Sigmoid (predicts RGB in [0, 1])

Training used Adam with learning rate 1e-2 and MSE loss.

Part 2. Neural radiance field on multi-view Lego

In this section I implemented a complete NeRF pipeline: converting image pixels to 3D world-space rays, sampling points along those rays, evaluating a radiance field network, and performing differentiable volume rendering to optimize the model against multiple posed RGB images.

2.1. Camera to ray pipeline

  • pixel_to_camera(K, uv): I convert pixel coordinates to camera coordinates by analytically inverting the pinhole intrinsics. For each pixel (u, v), I compute (x, y, z) = ((u - ox)/fx, (v - oy)/fy, 1).
  • transform(c2w, x_c): my world-space transform builds homogeneous coordinates explicitly. Given x_c of shape N×3, I append a column of ones, reshape to (N, 4, 1), then left-multiply by the camera-to-world matrix: c2w @ [x, y, z, 1]^T gives [X, Y, Z, 1]^T. The result is squeezed back to (N, 3). This matches the mathematical camera transform exactly and is fully vectorized across all points.
  • pixel_to_ray(K, c2w, uv): after converting pixel locations to camera coordinates and transforming them into world space with the two functions above, I compute ray_o = c2w[:3, 3], since the origin is simply the translation of the camera in the world frame, and ray_d = normalize(x_w - ray_o), since the ray direction is the difference between the world point and the camera position. This yields the origin and direction of each ray used during training.

Together, these steps implement the full pixel to camera to world to ray pipeline required for NeRF training.

2.2. Sampling rays and points

I use global ray sampling, where all pixels from all training images are flattened into a single pool. Each pixel is recorded with a +0.5 offset so that sampling occurs at the pixel center. At every iteration, I randomly choose N pixel indices, giving uniformly distributed rays across all views.

For each selected pixel, I compute its ray origin and direction with pixel_to_ray. Each ray is then discretized into n_samples = 64 points between near = 2.0 and far = 6.0 using x = r_o + r_d · t.

During training I make sure every portion of the ray is eventually sampled, and prevent the network from overfitting to fixed depths, by adding random noise within each interval (ti becomes ti + ε·Δt). Over training, every part of the ray contributes to the reconstruction. The values n_samples, near, and far are parameters in my implementation, and I later change them for different scenes (my real dataset uses a smaller range), since the optimal sampling bounds depend heavily on object scale and the physical capture setup.

2.3. Dataloader, precomputation, and Viser visualizations

To accelerate training, I built a preprocessing stage that:

  • enumerates every pixel in every image with a +0.5 center offset,
  • stores their RGB values,
  • stores a repeated copy of the camera extrinsic for each pixel,
  • precomputes world-space ray_o and ray_d for the entire dataset.

The dataloader then samples rays by indexing into these large flattened arrays. I validated UV alignment, ray orientation, and frustum consistency using Viser visualizations.

2.4. Neural radiance field MLP

My NeRF model closely follows the original architecture: a deep point MLP with a skip connection, a density head, and a separate view-dependent color head. Both 3D sample locations and ray directions use sinusoidal positional encoding before being fed into the network.

  • Positional encoding: 3D points are encoded with Lx = 10, giving 3 + 6L = 63 channels. Ray directions use Lr = 4, producing 3 + 6L = 27 channels.
  • Point MLP (σ and features): the encoded 3D point is processed by an 8-layer MLP of width 256. At the fourth hidden layer (index 3), I concatenate the original positional encoding back into the network, matching Linear(256 + input_dim_x, 256). This skip connection helps preserve high-frequency spatial detail.
  • Density head (σ): after the final point layer, a 256 → 1 linear projection followed by ReLU produces the volume density σ.
  • Color head (rgb): the point-MLP output is first transformed into a 256-dimensional feature vector. This is concatenated with the encoded ray direction (27-dimensional) and passed through (256 + input_dim_r) → 128 with ReLU, then 128 → 3 with Sigmoid to produce RGB in [0, 1].
  • Outputs: the network returns σ (density) and rgb (color).

2.5. Volume rendering

I implemented volume rendering entirely in vectorized form. For each ray, the sampled densities σ and colors rgb are combined using the weighting

w_i = T_i · (1 − exp(−σ_i Δt))

where the transmittance T_i is computed from a shifted cumulative sum (up to but excluding sample i) of the densities:

T_i = exp( − cumulative_sum(σ · Δt) )

This matches the continuous volume rendering equation while remaining efficient, since all rays and samples are processed in parallel. The final pixel color is

C = Σ w_i · rgb_i

Part 2.6. NeRF trained on my own captured object

For this section, I trained a full neural radiance field using the dataset captured in Part 0. This used my own calibrated images and camera poses, with a NeRF model identical in structure to Part 2 and adjustments to the near/far range and sampling strategy because of the much smaller physical scale of the scene.

Model architecture and training setup

I used a custom NeRF architecture with a skip connection, separate density and color branches, and independent positional-encoding frequencies for 3D sample points and view directions.

Hyperparameter and implementation adjustments

  • near = 0.2 and far = 0.5 (the real-world scene was much smaller than the Lego dataset)
  • 64 samples per ray, jittered during training
  • Positional encoding: Lx = 10 for positions, Lr = 4 for directions
  • Adam optimizer: lr = 5e-4

These adjustments were essential to avoid the model sampling empty space and to keep rays bounded within the tight capture volume around the object.
